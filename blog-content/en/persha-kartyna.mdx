A first purchase of painting differs from every later one in that there are no bearings at all. You have no taste of your own yet — that comes later. No sense of prices. No confidence that you are even entitled to judge.

That is normal. Here is what to do.

## The main mistake

Buying not what you like but what seems correct.

Someone reads that abstraction is currently valued and takes an abstract work, though they are drawn to the figurative. Or the reverse: they fear looking unmodern and pass over what genuinely struck them.

The result is the same: the work hangs, irritates, and within a year goes into a cupboard.

A first painting should be the one you want to look at. That is the only criterion that works without fail — everything else comes with experience.

## How to tell a work is good

There is no objective scale, but there are signs to steer by.

**The work withstands a long look.** A weak picture reads in three seconds and adds nothing after. A good one opens something each time that you had not noticed. This is the most reliable test: look at the work for three minutes and check whether it has become dull.

**The composition holds.** Cover part of the image with your hand. If the remainder falls apart, the composition is built. If nothing changes, it is accidental.

**Colour works together.** In a good work the colours are connected: they repeat, support one another, form a system. In a weak one they are simply present.

**There is surface.** Not in the sense of paint thickness but in the sense of a visible hand. A brushstroke that decides something rather than merely filling the plane.

**The work does not explain itself.** If a painting needs a paragraph of text to function, something is wrong with the painting.

## What budget to allow

A realistic start for contemporary European painting is [eight hundred to two thousand euros](/en/blog/skilky-koshtuye-kartyna) for a medium-format work.

Below that line you will usually find studies, prints or work by beginners. That is a fine entry point, but such a purchase makes it hard to learn whether painting suits you — the chance of getting something weak is too high.

Above two thousand is also fine, if the work genuinely struck you. For a first purchase, though, it is not necessary.

What certainly not to do: take the cheapest available simply because it is cheapest. Price is not the main criterion here, but a work that is too cheap almost always means you are buying decoration rather than painting.

## The sequence

**1. Look at many, buy one.** Before a first purchase it is worth seeing hundreds of works: in galleries, on artists' websites, in museums. Taste forms from volume, not from reading.

**2. Choose three or four candidates.** Not one — three. Comparison reveals differences invisible in a single work.

**3. See the original if you can.** A screen conveys composition and colour but not surface. The difference between an original and its photograph is greater than you expect.

**4. [Try the size](/en/blog/rozmir-kartyny-dlya-interyeru).** Cut a rectangle of the intended dimensions from paper, tape it to the wall, live with it for a few days.

**5. Settle everything before paying.** Dimensions without the frame, whether it is varnished, whether it is ready to hang, how it will be packed, what happens with the documents.

**6. Take the documents at once.** Certificate, invoice, photograph of the work.

## What not to do

**Do not buy in a single meeting.** If the seller presses with a deadline, a "today only" discount, or the claim that the work is about to go — that is the wrong seller.

**[Do not buy for a specific sofa](/en/blog/kartyna-v-interyeri).** Furniture changes faster than painting. A work bought to match upholstery becomes redundant at the first refurbishment.

**Do not follow fashion.** What appears in interiors magazines now will look dated in five years. A work bought because you like it will not.

**Do not be afraid to ask.** You can ask an artist or a gallery anything: why this price, how long the work took, which cycle it belongs to. Reluctance to answer is a signal.

## Where to buy a first work

**[Directly from the artist](/en/blog/yak-kupyty-kartynu-napryamu).** The cheapest entry: no gallery markup, which runs from thirty to fifty percent. Plus the chance to talk to the author, see other works, understand the context.

**From a gallery.** More expensive, but the gallery takes on selection and guarantees. For a first purchase that is justified if you have no bearings at all.

**At auction.** Not advisable for a first purchase: it requires market knowledge, and without it you can easily overpay or acquire a problematic work.

**On a marketplace.** With caution. Much of what is sold as painting is print or furniture-grade decoration.
