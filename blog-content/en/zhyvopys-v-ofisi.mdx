Office walls are usually taken up with one of two things: nothing, or posters with motivational slogans. Both say more about the company than it would like.

An original painting says something else. Here is where it belongs and what it gives.

## What a visitor reads

Someone entering a meeting room draws conclusions about the company within seconds, before the first word. The surroundings are part of those conclusions.

**Empty walls** read as impermanence. The company has either just moved in, or does not intend to stay, or does not care how it looks.

**A poster with a slogan** reads as an attempt to seem. "Success", "Teamwork", mountains and eagles — a declaration of values nobody in the room believes in, including whoever hung it.

**An original painting** reads as stability and taste. A company that bought a real work rather than printing an image is one that is here for the long term and attends to detail.

It is not even about art. It is about the signal the wall sends before the conversation begins.

## Where it works

**The meeting room.** The main place. This is where clients, partners and candidates are received. One strong work on the wall behind the person leading the meeting does more than any branding.

**Reception, the waiting area.** People wait and look at the walls. Painting turns waiting from dead time into an impression.

**The director's office.** A classic place, and a justified one: a work in the office of the head of the company reads as a personal choice rather than a corporate decision.

**Corridors, halls.** Usually dead space. Painting brings it to life and sets the tone for a whole floor.

## What to choose

An office has its own particulars, and they narrow the choice.

**Avoid the personal and the disturbing.** At home you can hang a work with a heavy subject — it is your space. In an office the work should be one a stranger at a business meeting is comfortable with.

**A restrained palette works better.** A bright, dominant work pulls attention away and hinders conversation. A muted palette creates atmosphere without imposing.

**Figurative is safer than abstract.** In a meeting room an abstract work can seem cold or pretentious. Figurative painting reads warmer and more human.

**Size to the space.** In a meeting room with a 3-metre ceiling a small work is lost. Here you need a format from a metre up, or a group.

**Neutral in subject.** Painting in an office should declare nothing. Works that are simply good, without a direct message, work best.

## Painting as a partner gift

A separate matter — a painting given from one company to another.

The standard corporate gift is a set, a branded object, a bottle. All of it is forgotten within a week and differs in nothing from what everyone gives.

An original painting is remembered. It hangs in an office or meeting room for years, and every time it is looked at, people recall who gave it. It is the longest advertising contact a company can buy.

Plus the signal: a company that gives an original rather than a trinket is one that knows value and does not economise on relationships.

The certificate can be issued with the occasion and the giving company's name — the connection then documented.

## Practical matters

**Who decides.** In business, choosing a painting often gets stuck between departments. It is simplest when one person decides — the head, or whoever they delegated it to. A committee to choose painting rarely reaches a result.

**Budget.** An original of medium format runs eight hundred to two thousand euros. For a company this is an ordinary line in fit-out costs, comparable to furniture for the meeting room.

**Framing and delivery.** A work on a stretcher is ready to hang. Delivery and installation are arranged separately. For large formats a secure wall fixing should be provided.

**Documents.** The company receives an invoice and a certificate. The painting can be entered as an asset — a matter for the accountants, but painting is suited to it.
