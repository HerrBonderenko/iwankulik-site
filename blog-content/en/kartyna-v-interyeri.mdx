The question "which painting will suit my interior" comes up constantly, and it is the wrong question.

The right one runs differently: how do I keep the interior from getting in the painting's way.

The difference is not verbal. In the first case painting is an addition to furniture. In the second it is what the space exists for. The second gives a result that does not go stale within a year.

## Why not to match the sofa

Three reasons, each practical.

**Furniture changes faster.** A sofa lasts seven to ten years; upholstery is replaced more often still. An oil painting lasts centuries. A work bought to match the cushions becomes redundant at the first change of decor.

**An exact match looks cheap.** When the colour of the painting repeats the colour of the upholstery, both objects read as parts of one suite. The painting stops being a separate object and becomes an element of decor.

**It narrows the choice to the accidental.** Searching for "something in grey-blue tones" cuts off ninety percent of the good works and leaves you choosing among the remaining ten — not by quality but by colour.

## What actually matters

**Overall colour temperature.** Not the specific shade but warmth or coolness. A warm work — ochre, sienna, terracotta — will fit a room with wood, warm light, natural fabrics. A cool one — blue-green, grey — demands the right surroundings and will look foreign on a warm wall.

This is the one parameter worth coordinating. The rest is freedom.

**The wall's brightness.** A dark work on a dark wall in a dim room will simply disappear. A light work on a light wall dissolves. Contrast is needed in at least one direction.

**Viewing distance.** A large canvas needs room to step back. In a narrow corridor a 150 cm work does not read — you see it in fragments.

## Walls

**White walls** — the safest option. Works with any palette, does not argue, lets the work be the main thing. The downside: in a large room white walls can feel cold.

**Warm neutrals** — milk, sand, light grey with a warm undertone. Best for oil painting: they support warm works and do not kill cool ones.

**Dark walls** — deep graphite, dark green, blue. Painting on them looks as it does in a museum under directed light: the colour deepens, the work gains weight. But good lighting is needed, or the painting vanishes.

**Coloured walls** — the riskiest. A saturated wall competes with the painting. If the wall is already strong, the work must be chosen so that it outweighs that strength rather than contesting it.

**Patterned wallpaper** — the worst solution for painting. Wall pattern and pictorial composition always interfere with each other.

## Interior styles

**Minimalism.** Painting has room here: empty walls, restrained furniture, the work becomes the only event in the room. Almost anything fits, but dense and complex compositions gain most — they have something with which to fill the silence.

**Scandinavian.** Pale wood, white walls, soft light. A warm palette works naturally. Cool works do too, but they need more space around them.

**Classical.** A frame is more appropriate here than a gallery wrap. Framed painting reads as part of a tradition rather than a contemporary statement.

**Loft.** Brick, concrete, metal. Painting on a brick wall looks good, but brick is already texture, so the work must be either very dense in colour or large in format. A small canvas is lost on brick.

**Eclectic.** The most rewarding setting. Here painting does not have to match anything — it becomes another voice in the conversation of objects.

## Lighting

**Natural.** Diffuse side light is best. Direct sun destroys paintings, so the wall facing the window is a poor idea however much light there is.

**Artificial.** LED fittings at a warm temperature, 2700–3000 K. They do not heat and emit no ultraviolet.

**Directed light.** A dedicated fitting above the painting changes it entirely. This is what galleries do, and at home it works the same. Angle around 30 degrees so that the varnish does not reflect.

**What not to do.** Do not place a halogen lamp close to the canvas — it heats. Do not aim light perpendicular to the surface — you will get a mirror reflection.

## When they do not get along

It happens: the work is good, the interior is good, together they do not function.

**Change the wall, not the work.** Repainting a wall in a neutral tone is cheaper than selling the painting and buying another.

**Give it more air.** Often the problem is that there is too much around the work. Take the photographs, the shelf and the clock off the same wall — and the painting begins to breathe.

**Rehang it.** The same work on a different wall can look entirely different because of light and what surrounds it.

**Wait.** Sometimes a painting is not accepted simply because it is new. After two or three months the eye adjusts and the work becomes part of the house.
