The most common mistake when buying a painting is taking one that is too small. It seems that a large canvas would be “too much”, that it would take over the room. On the wall the opposite happens: a small painting gets lost, looks accidental, and instead of a focal point you get a patch of indeterminate purpose.

Here are the concrete numbers for typical situations.

## The two-thirds rule

The basic rule of hanging: a painting above furniture should occupy two-thirds to three-quarters of its width.

A 200 cm sofa — a work of 130 to 150 cm. A 120 cm chest of drawers — 80 to 90 cm. A 160 cm bed — 100 to 120 cm.

The logic is simple: below two-thirds the work looks lost; above three-quarters it feels cramped, as though the painting were pressing against the edges of the furniture.

If you are hanging a group of works, apply the rule to the whole group including the gaps between them.

## Hanging height

The centre of the painting belongs at eye level — roughly 145–155 cm from the floor.

That is the museum standard, and it works the same way in a home. The typical mistake is hanging too high: people instinctively take their cue from the ceiling rather than from eye level.

The exception is hanging above furniture. Here the bottom edge should sit 15–25 cm above the back of the sofa or the top of the chest. Less, and the work “sits” on the furniture; more, and the connection between them is lost.

In a dining room, where people are mostly seated, you can go 10 cm lower than usual.

## Specific situations

**Above a sofa.** The most common case. A sofa of 180–220 cm — a work 120–150 cm wide. Landscape format works better than portrait: it echoes the line of the furniture.

**Above a bed.** A 160 cm bed — a work of 100–120 cm. Landscape or square suits better here. A portrait-format work above a headboard looks unbalanced.

**Above a chest or console.** A work roughly the width of the furniture or slightly narrower. Portrait format is appropriate here — it offsets the horizontal of the furniture.

**An empty wall with no furniture.** The hardest case, because there is no reference point. The guide: the work should occupy a third to a half of the wall's width. A 3-metre wall — a canvas of 100–150 cm.

**Narrow spaces: hallway, pier wall.** Portrait format, narrow and tall. 50 × 70 or 60 × 90 cm.

**Above a staircase.** Hang on the diagonal, following the line of the flight. Works of equal size, centres at equal distance from the steps.

## A group of works

If you are hanging several canvases together, a few pointers.

**Spacing between works** — 5 to 10 cm. Less and they merge into a single patch; more and they fall apart into separate objects.

**Equal sizes** are simpler: a two-by-two grid or a row of three.

**Unequal sizes** need an axis. Align all the works either along the bottom edge or along the horizontal centre line. Aligning along the top edge looks worse.

**An odd number** in a row reads more calmly than an even one. Three works are better than two, five better than four.

## What else to consider

**Viewing distance.** A large work needs room to step back. In a small room you will not see a 150 cm canvas whole — you will read it in parts. Rule of thumb: the viewing distance should be about twice the diagonal of the work.

**Lighting.** Direct sun destroys paintings, so [the wall opposite a window is a bad idea](/en/blog/de-vishaty-kartynu). Diffuse side light or a directed LED fitting is best.

**[The room's palette](/en/blog/kartyna-v-interyeri).** A work in warm ochre tones fits almost anywhere. A cool blue-green needs the right surroundings and will look foreign on a warm wall.

**Ceiling height.** In rooms with high ceilings — from 3 metres — small works are lost entirely. There you need a large format or a group.

## Quick reference

| Where | Furniture width | Work width |
|---|---|---|
| Above a sofa | 180–220 cm | 120–150 cm |
| Above a bed | 140–180 cm | 100–120 cm |
| Above a chest | 100–140 cm | 80–110 cm |
| Empty wall | 3 m | 100–150 cm |
| Narrow space | — | 50 × 70, 60 × 90 |
