The question of framing arrives right after the purchase and almost always catches people off guard. You spent a month choosing the work and five minutes deciding on the frame at the framer's — and then live with that decision for years.

Here is what it consists of.

## Two basic options

**Gallery wrap.** The canvas is stretched over a bar 3–4 cm deep, with the painting continuing round the sides. The work hangs as it is, unframed. Looks contemporary, requires nothing further, ready to hang immediately.

**A frame.** The work is set into a wooden moulding. The classical method, centuries old. It adds weight — physical and visual.

Both options are correct. The question is what a particular work is doing in a particular space.

## When a frame is needed

**A work on a thin stretcher.** At 1.5–2 cm deep the side looks unfinished. Here the frame solves a technical problem, not a decorative one.

**A classical interior.** In a space with mouldings, herringbone parquet and wooden furniture a gallery wrap looks foreign. A frame ties the work to its surroundings.

**A work that needs setting apart.** On a busy wall or among other objects a frame creates a boundary within which the painting exists separately.

**A composition that runs to the edges.** If the image breaks off at the edge of the canvas, a frame gives it support. A composition with margins in the centre of the canvas does not need this.

**Small format.** A work of 30 × 40 cm is lost on a wall. A frame adds 10–15 cm on each side and gives it presence.

## When a frame harms

**A contemporary work in a contemporary interior.** Minimalism, loft, Scandinavian style — a frame is superfluous here, adding weight nobody asked for.

**A work painted round the sides.** If the artist carried the image onto the edge, that was a decision. A frame conceals it.

**A very large canvas.** A work of 150 × 200 cm in a wide moulding becomes a monument. Plus the weight: such a frame adds several kilograms, and the fixing has to be recalculated.

**When the frame costs more than the work.** This happens more often than you would think. A good moulding for a large format costs as much as a medium painting. On a limited budget the money is better spent on the painting.

## Types of frame

**Narrow profile**, 2–4 cm. Universal. Does not argue with the work, provides a boundary, draws no attention. The safest choice for contemporary painting.

**Wide classical**, from 6 cm. For a work that can carry that weight. Looks expensive but easily pulls attention onto itself.

**Floater frame.** The canvas sits with a 5–10 mm gap from the moulding and appears to hover inside it. The best contemporary option: the frame is there, the pressure is not. More expensive than a standard one.

**No moulding, just an edge strip.** A thin wooden or metal border. Minimum presence, maximum neatness.

## Material and colour

**Wood** — the basic material. Oak, ash, beech. Can be stained, painted, patinated.

**Metal** — aluminium profile. Contemporary, slim, suits loft and minimalism. Cheaper than wood.

**Colour.** Three working strategies:

Neutral — black, white, natural wood. Does not intervene, suits almost always.

Supporting — the frame picks up one of the work's colours, but muted. Ties the composition together.

Contrasting — the frame opposes the work in temperature. Risky, but powerful when it lands.

What not to do: choose a frame to match the furniture. The frame belongs to the painting, not to the interior.

## Glass

For oil painting **glass is not needed**. More than that, it does harm: under glass the exchange of air is impeded, and the varnish layer needs access to oxygen.

Glass goes over prints, watercolour, pastel — anything without a protective coating.

If you nonetheless want glass over oil, it must be non-reflective museum glass with a spacer from the surface. That is expensive and almost always unnecessary.

## What it costs

Price range for a work of 80 × 100 cm.

| Type of presentation | Cost range |
|---|---|
| Gallery wrap (included in the price) | €0 |
| Narrow aluminium profile | €60–120 |
| Simple wooden frame | €100–250 |
| Floater frame | €180–350 |
| Wide classical frame | €250–600 |

For large formats from 120 cm the prices roughly double.

## Practical points

**Do not rush.** A work can hang unframed for months. Live with it and understand what is missing.

**Take the work to the framer.** Choosing a moulding from a photograph is pointless. The framer will hold several samples against the actual canvas and the decision becomes obvious in a minute.

**Check the weight.** A frame adds kilograms. A standard plasterboard fixing will hold the work unframed but may not hold it framed.

**Fixing to the frame, not the canvas.** The hanging hardware belongs on the frame moulding. Loading the stretcher through the frame is the classic error of cheap framing.
