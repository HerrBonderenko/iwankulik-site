Oil painting responds to conservation better than any other technique. This is not rhetoric: watercolour, pastel and prints are often lost for good after serious damage, while oil on canvas can be recovered even after a tear.

Here is what can be done with what.

## What can be fully repaired

**Darkened varnish.** The commonest case and the simplest. The conservator removes the old layer with solvent and applies a new one. The painting beneath is unchanged — provided the varnish was of good quality and comes off as intended.

After this treatment a work looks as though it were painted yesterday. That is exactly why seventeenth-century museum canvases do not look their age.

**Surface grime.** Dust, soot, tobacco film. Removed together with the varnish or separately.

**A tear in the canvas.** It sounds alarming but is repaired reliably. The conservator lines the canvas — attaching new fabric to the back — then retouches the loss in the paint layer. Carefully done, the join is invisible.

**Slackened tension.** The canvas has sagged and waves have formed. The simplest case: tap out the keys in the corners or restretch the work.

## What can be partly repaired

**Cracks in the paint layer.** Here it depends on the cause. Fine age crackle is a natural process; it is not removed but consolidated so that the paint does not flake further.

Deep cracks from technical faults — [layers built up incorrectly](/en/blog/materialy-hudozhnyka) — cannot be eliminated. They are stabilised and retouched, but the structure remains.

**Paint loss.** Areas where the paint layer has lifted and fallen away. The conservator consolidates the edges and retouches the loss. But retouching is not original painting: it is executed so as to be identifiable under specialist examination, and by the rules it must be reversible.

**Water damage.** Stains, distortion of the canvas, sometimes mould on the reverse. Mould is removed, stains partly, distortion corrected by restretching. But if the damp stood for a long time, the canvas may have lost strength.

## What cannot be repaired

**Faded pigments.** If a work [hung for years in direct sun](/en/blog/de-vishaty-kartynu), some colours have bleached. That is not grime or film — the pigment itself has changed chemically. It cannot be brought back; one can only paint over, and that is no longer the original.

**Large areas of loss.** If a palm-sized piece of the painting has gone, the conservator can reconstruct it from photographs or by analogy. But it will be a reconstruction, not the original, and the work's value falls accordingly.

**The consequences of bad restoration.** The worst case. A work once “refreshed” with oil paint over the original, or cleaned with an aggressive solvent that took the upper glazes with it. There is nothing to repair here — the original is already lost.

## When to do nothing

**Fine crackle.** That is the patina of age, not a defect. Museum practice does not remove it.

**Slight yellowing of varnish on a warm-toned work.** Sometimes it even suits the painting. [Varnish is renewed](/en/blog/lak-na-kartyni) when the yellowing makes the colour unreadable, not by the calendar.

**Small abrasions at the edges.** If they are not spreading, better left alone. Any intervention is a risk.

**A work you do not intend to sell.** Conservation costs money and always alters the object slightly. If the painting hangs in your home and you are content — leave it be.

## What it costs

Price range for a work of 80 × 100 cm. Prices vary considerably by country and by the conservator's standing.

| Treatment | Cost range |
|---|---|
| Varnish removal and reapplication | €150–400 |
| Retensioning, replacing keys | €50–150 |
| Lining the canvas | €300–800 |
| Retouching small losses | from €100 per area |
| Full conservation treatment | from €800 |

A rule worth keeping: if conservation costs more than half the value of the work, consider whether it is needed.

## How to choose a conservator

**Specialist training.** Painting conservation is its own profession, not the same as being a painter. A good painter can ruin a work while acting with the best intentions.

**Experience with comparable works.** An icon conservator and a conservator of contemporary painting work differently.

**Reversibility of intervention.** The founding principle of modern conservation: everything done must be removable without harm to the original. Anyone offering to “refresh” a work with oil paint is breaking this rule.

**Photographic documentation.** Normal practice is images before, during and after treatment, with a description of what was done.

**An estimate before starting.** The conservator should examine the work and state scope and cost in advance.

## What to do when damage occurs

**Do not attempt repairs yourself.** The commonest cause of irreversible loss is an attempt to wipe, glue or paint over it personally.

**Photograph it.** The whole work and the damage close up.

**Stabilise the state.** If paint is flaking, lay the work flat face up so that fragments are not lost.

**Contact a conservator.** If the artist is alive, go to him first: he knows the technology of that particular work and can recommend someone.
