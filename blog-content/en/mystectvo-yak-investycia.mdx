A great deal is said about art as an investment, and most of it is dishonest. So let me start with what this piece will not contain: promises of returns, comparisons with gold, and charts of art market growth over twenty years.

Those charts exist, but they describe the upper segment — works worth hundreds of thousands of euros passing through the major auction houses. They have no bearing on buying a painting for two thousand.

Let us look at what actually happens to the value of painting, and what can reasonably be expected.

## Why painting is not a financial instrument

Three reasons nobody likes to state.

**No liquidity.** A share can be sold in seconds. A painting takes months, and only if a buyer appears. A fast sale almost always means selling for less than you paid.

**No quoted price.** The value of a particular work is unknown until it has been bought. Expert valuations and prices for similar works give a bearing, no more.

**Holding costs.** Insurance, storage conditions, occasionally conservation. Not much, but not nothing, and a financial instrument ought to yield rather than cost.

The honest formulation, then: painting is not an investment. It is an object that can hold its value and, in favourable circumstances, increase it.

## What actually affects value

**The artist's reputation.** The main and almost the only factor. It grows with new exhibitions, publications, works entering notable collections, mentions in the press. If a painter keeps working and stays visible, his works appreciate. If not, they stand still.

**The work's place in the body of work.** A work from a known cycle is valued above a random canvas. A key work of a series above an incidental one.

**Condition.** An undamaged canvas with unaltered varnish on its original stretcher is worth more than the same one after an unsuccessful restoration.

**Documented provenance.** A work with a certificate from the artist and a known chain of ownership sells faster and for more than one without papers. On the secondary market this is the decisive difference.

## Where to begin

**Buy what you are prepared to live with.** This is not sentimental advice but practical. The work will hang in your home for years. If it was bought "for appreciation" and you do not like it, you have acquired an illiquid asset that also irritates you daily.

**Buy directly where possible.** The gallery markup runs from thirty to fifty percent. On the secondary market it has to be earned back before you break even. Buying at the studio means entering at the artist's price.

**Take the documents at once.** Certificate, invoice, photograph of the work. In ten years this will be harder to reconstruct, and once the artist is gone, impossible.

**Do not rush.** The first purchase need not be the most expensive. It is wiser to take a medium-format work, live with it for a year, and understand what actually interests you.

## What to avoid

**Works "for the interior" from furniture showrooms.** That is decoration, not painting, and has no value by definition.

**Editioned prints without numbering.** If no impression number and edition size are given, it is simply a print.

**Promises of returns.** A seller who quotes a growth percentage either does not understand the market or is counting on you not understanding it.

**Buying the name rather than the work.** A known artist is no guarantee: every painter has strong pieces and incidental ones. The latter do not appreciate.

## What it costs at the start

Contemporary European figurative painting of medium format runs from eight hundred to two thousand euros for a work of 60 × 80 cm. A large canvas of 100 × 140 cm starts from two and a half thousand.

At this order of magnitude a collection of five or six works can be assembled over several years without altering your way of life.

The upper segment — from tens of thousands — follows a different logic and has a different audience. It does function as a financial instrument, but entry begins at sums that make this conversation beside the point.

## On the time horizon

Painting does not work over short intervals. A work bought and sold two years later will almost certainly show a loss — for the same reasons: holding costs and lack of liquidity.

A sensible horizon starts at ten years. Over that period the artist's reputation either grows or it does not, and that becomes evident.

Over that same period you will either come to love the work or realise you were mistaken. And the former happens more often when the choice was made not by forecast but by what struck you.
