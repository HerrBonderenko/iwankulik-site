Orwell wrote *Animal Farm* in 1945 and for a long time could not get it published. The refusals had one reason: the book seemed inconvenient at a moment when inconvenience was unwelcome.

Eighty years later the book remains, and the inconvenience people shied away from has become obvious to everyone. That is what happens to parables: they are written about the specific and they work against anything.

## What it is actually about

The animals drive out the farmer and build a just farm. Then, step by step, justice turns into the very thing they fled, only under different names.

What matters in the text is not the plot but how imperceptibly the wording changes. First: "All animals are equal." Then the same sentence, with an addition that inverts everything. Nobody notices the moment when one became the other, because each individual step was small.

This is not about one country. It is about a mechanism that engages every time someone granted the right to decide for others gradually stops seeing them as equals.

## Why it can be painted

Because Orwell supplied a ready-made visual metaphor, and it works better in painting than in prose.

In the closing scene the animals look through the window at the pigs sitting at a table with the men, and can no longer tell which is which. In the book that is the last sentence. In painting it can be the whole picture.

The cycle began with a simple observation: people and animals in my paintings had long been holding the same postures. I noticed this before I connected it to Orwell — the figures in my work sit, lean and reach in the same way, regardless of whom they depict.

Orwell simply gave it a name.

## What is in the cycle

**Animals with human gazes.** Not caricature, not cartoon. A face constructed so that a human expression reads in it — doubt, calculation, fatigue.

**People with animal features.** The reverse movement. Not horns or tails, but something in the proportions and in the way a body is held.

**The blurred line.** In many works it is impossible to say with certainty whom you are looking at. This is not an error or a lack of finish — it is the main thing I want to show.

## The table

The recurring motif of the cycle is the table.

Deals are made across it. Things are divided across it. Those who decide sit at it; those decided about stand beside it or are not shown at all.

The table is convenient because it establishes hierarchy immediately, without explanation. Who is behind it, who is in front of it, who watches from the side. No caption and no plot are needed — it is enough to place the figures.

In Orwell's ending the table is precisely where the line disappears for good. The pigs sit with the men, and from outside they are already indistinguishable.

## How this differs from book illustration

Classic illustrations to *Animal Farm* follow the plot: the rebellion, Napoleon, the windmill, the seven commandments on the barn wall.

I am not interested in enumerating episodes. In my work there is no barn, no commandments, no scenes from the book. There are not even pigs in any literal sense — the animals in the cycle vary, and the particular species usually does not matter.

What there is, is the situation. Two or three figures at a table, the distribution of roles legible from their postures, and the viewer decides who is who. Often it turns out that it cannot be decided.

## Connection to the other cycles

*Animal Farm* stands closest to *Critique of Power* — the same subject, only without a literary source.

The difference is in tone. In *Critique of Power* the figures are nameless and the situation is presented directly. In *Animal Farm* there is a layer of satire that allows a sharper statement without slipping into journalism. It is the same device Orwell used: a parable permits saying what cannot be said outright.

There is [kinship with Stańczyk](/en/blog/stanczyk-u-zhyvopysi) too. A jester at court is someone who sits at the table on the terms of furniture. He sees everything, and his presence disturbs no one.

## On materials

The works in the cycle are predominantly horizontal — the table demands the horizontal.

Formats run from medium to large. A scene with several figures stops reading at small size: for the postures to work, space is needed.

The technique is the same: oil on linen, [multi-layered painting](/en/blog/oliya-na-polotni-tehnika). In this cycle the palette is mostly muted and earthy — bright colour would be superfluous here, it would turn satire into caricature.
