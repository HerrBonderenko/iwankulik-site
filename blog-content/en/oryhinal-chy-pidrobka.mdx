Three different things are often confused, and most misunderstandings start there.

**A reproduction** is a print of an image on paper or imitation canvas. Sold as a reproduction, priced accordingly, deceiving no one.

**A forgery** is a work presented as the creation of another artist. It may be hand-painted and quite good, but attributed to someone other than its maker.

**An original** is a work made by the person to whom it is attributed.

The first is easy to distinguish. The second is harder but also open to checking. In order, then.

## Raking light settles everything

The simplest test, requiring no equipment.

Take the work and look at it at a sharp angle, with light falling from the side. Best at a window on an overcast day, turning the canvas so that the light skims the surface.

**An oil original** has relief. The brushstrokes stand proud of the plane, there are hollows between them, in places the paint lies thickly. The shadow cast by the strokes is visible to the naked eye.

**A print** is flat. Even where an imitation texture gel has been applied on top, it will not coincide with the brushstrokes of the image. The gel is applied mechanically and repeats a general relief, not a specific movement of the brush.

This is the most reliable of the simple checks. It cannot be circumvented cheaply.

## The edge of the canvas

Look at the side — where the canvas wraps around the stretcher.

**On an original** the paint runs over the edge unevenly. There are accidental drips, places where the brush caught the edge, and stretches the paint never reached. The painter paints the front; the edge happens by itself.

**On a print** the edge is either clean and sharp or the image simply stops at the corner. Sometimes a continuation of the image is printed on the side — and it looks suspiciously tidy.

## The reverse

The most informative part of a work, and the one least often examined.

**The canvas.** On an original, the ground and the weave are visible. In places the paint has pushed through the fabric — normal with dense painting. The weave has natural irregularity.

**The stretcher.** Wooden, [with keys in the corners](/en/blog/materialy-hudozhnyka). There are usually handwritten notes on it: title, year, sometimes a catalogue number. Wood darkens over time.

**The stretching.** On an original, staples or tacks sit unevenly, at varying intervals. Machine stretching is regular, at a constant pitch — characteristic of canvas prints.

**The smell.** A fresh oil painting smells — of oil and solvent — for several weeks after completion. A print smells of nothing, apart from possibly varnish.

## The signature

A signature is not proof in itself, but it yields useful information.

**Painted in oil**, it has the same surface as the painting. It stands proud, the movement of the brush is visible in it, the line varies in thickness.

**Printed**, it lies in the plane of the image, has no relief, and the line is of constant thickness.

**Added later** is a separate case. A signature on top of the varnish looks different from one beneath it: it catches the light differently and sometimes differs in tone. This is a classic sign of falsified attribution.

## What is harder to verify

Everything above distinguishes hand-work from print. It does not distinguish a particular artist's original from a good hand-painted forgery.

For that, something else is needed.

**Provenance.** Who owned it previously, where the work came from, whether documents exist. A chain that ends at "inherited from family" is weak.

**[A certificate from a living artist](/en/blog/sertyfikat-avtentychnosti).** The strongest document. The painter confirms his own work, and only he can dispute it.

**The catalogue of works.** If the artist keeps a numbered record, the work can be checked against it.

**An expert opinion.** Needed when the author is unavailable. An art historian examines style, materials and technique. It costs money and does not always yield an unambiguous conclusion.

## The commonest patterns

**A print sold as an original.** The most widespread, and the easiest to detect with raking light.

**Hand-work with someone else's signature.** Painted by an unknown hand, signed with a known name. Detected by expert opinion or comparison with documented works.

**A signature added later.** The painting is genuine; the attribution is not. Visible from the state of the varnish around the signature.

**"From the artist's studio."** A phrase that means nothing. The work may be by a pupil, an assistant, or have no connection to the master at all.

## How direct purchase removes all this

Every check above is needed when a third party stands between you and the artist.

[Buying at the studio](/en/blog/yak-kupyty-kartynu-napryamu), the question never arises: you see the work where it was made, you speak with the person who made it, and you take it away with a certificate signed in your presence.

The chain of ownership starts with you. Later, should the work reach the secondary market, its provenance will be documented from the first point — which is exactly what that market scrutinises most closely.
