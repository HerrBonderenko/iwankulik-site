The question comes up more than any other: why paint seven hundred works from one novel when you could paint a single good one.

The answer is not obvious, and it is not about quantity.

## Why one canvas is not enough

A painting shows one state. One composition, one lighting, one resolution. That is a great deal, but it is a fixed point.

A subject worth the trouble does not exhaust itself in a fixed point. It has range: the same question can be asked from ten sides, and each will give a different answer. A single work cuts off nine of them.

When you paint a series, something appears that does not exist in a single picture — movement between works. The viewer sees not only each canvas but the distance between them, and that distance says as much as the paintings themselves.

## What returning gives

**The first work on a subject is almost always wrong.** Not bad — simply made before it became clear what it was about. Understanding arrives in the process, and by the end of the first canvas it is already different from what it was at the start.

**The second corrects the first.** The third corrects the second correction. Somewhere around the fifth or sixth work the subject begins to open by itself, without effort — which means the right angle has been found.

**Ten years later the angle changes again.** You have become someone else, and what you saw in the text at forty reads differently at sixty. A work on the same subject turns out to be not a repetition but a different work.

## How a cycle differs from repeating yourself

This is the main danger. A painter who has found a successful device and begun to multiply it is also, formally speaking, working in series.

The difference lies in what changes.

**In repetition** the details change: a different colour, a different turn of the head, a different background. The construction stays the same. Such works can be painted endlessly and quickly, they sell well, and in ten years a hundred identical canvases accumulate.

**In a cycle** the question itself changes. A work painted in 2005 and one from 2020 on the same novel have different constructions, because they ask different questions.

The test is simple: if, looking at two works from a series, you cannot say how the second differs from the first in substance — it is not a cycle.

## How a subject sustains twenty years

Not every one does. Most exhaust themselves after three or four works, and that is normal: it means they had no depth, only a good subject matter.

The ones that hold are those containing a contradiction without resolution.

[*The Master and Margarita*](/en/blog/majster-i-margaryta-cykl): the force that punishes baseness is itself evil, and the novel will not let you settle whose side you are on. That cannot be resolved — one can only return to it.

[*Don Quixote*](/en/blog/don-kihot-cykl): a man mistaken about the world behaves with more dignity than those who are right about it. Here too there is no resolution.

A subject without contradiction is just a plot. It lasts for one work.

## How many works are in a cycle

This is not planned.

*The Master and Margarita* has about seven hundred, and not because I set myself that target. The novel simply turned out to be broader than the others: it has three layers of time and several dozen characters, each of which can be its own line.

The other cycles have fewer works — not because they are lesser, but because their range is narrower.

The number says nothing here. A cycle of twenty strong canvases weighs more than a cycle of two hundred repeating the same thing.

## What the viewer sees of this

A work can be looked at outside its cycle — it must stand alone, that is a condition.

But the cycle gives what a single work cannot: depth of acquaintance. Having seen three works from one series, you begin to notice what is constant and what changes. The constant turns out to be the subject; the variable turns out to be the questions put to it.

It is like the difference between meeting someone and talking with them over years. Neither is worse than the other, they are simply different.

## What this means when buying

The practical side, rarely discussed.

A work from a continuing cycle is part of a growing whole. Its value is tied not only to itself but to how the series and the artist's reputation develop.

A work from a closed cycle has its own logic: it belongs to a finished body, and its place within it is already settled.

Both are fine. But when buying it is worth understanding what the canvas belongs to — it affects both the reading and the work's later history.
